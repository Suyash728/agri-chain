2: Literature Review
[116] (normal) 2.1 Blockchain-Based Supply Chain Traceability
[117] (normal) Dudczyk, Dunston, and Crosby (2024) presented a comprehensive survey of blockchain technology for global supply chain management. Their study examined blockchain applications, available platforms, benefits, challenges, and future research opportunities. Blockchain provides a decentralized record of supply chain activities, supporting transparency and traceability among multiple stakeholders. However, the study covers supply chains across different industries and does not specifically evaluate the validation of agricultural sensor data before recording it on a blockchain. Aggarwal, Rani, Rani, and Sharma (2024) proposed a blockchain-based approach to agri-food supply chain traceability integrated with navigation technologies. Their research combines product tracking, geographical information, smart contracts, and distributed stor-age to improve food provenance and consumer trust. The integration of location information with blockchain records is particularly relevant to agricultural transportation. However, the increasing number of product records and the requirements of large-scale deployment present challenges for scalability and system performance.
[118] (normal) Wu, Jiang, and Cao (2023) investigated the efficiency of blockchain-based supply chain traceability.  Their approach uses parallel searching across multiple record chunks to improve retrieval efficiency as the volume of supply chain records increases. The study highlights that traceability systems must consider not only the reliability of records but also the time and storage required to retrieve them. Nevertheless, its primary emphasis is on recordsearch efficiency rather than identifying faulty or anomalous sensor readings before those readings enter the traceability process. Chaker and Damak (2024) discussed the use of blockchain to improve transparency, security, and traceability in supply chains. Their work explains how decentralization, cryp-tographic mechanisms, and smart contracts can support trustworthy interactions among participants. These capabilities provide a foundation for designing supply chain systems in which product information and ownership transfers can be recorded systematically. How-ever, blockchain-based record management alone does not establish whether the original data supplied to the system is physically accurate or reliable. Yavaprabhas, Kurnia, Seyedghorban, and Samson (2024) examined the relationship between blockchain adoption and trust in organic food supply chains. Their empirical case study indicates that blockchain-related transparency can help suppliers demonstrate trust-worthiness when establishing new business relationships. However, its impact may be more limited in already established relationships. The study also focuses on organic food supply chains, indicating the importance of considering different operational settings when applying blockchain-based traceability approaches. Overall, these studies establish the importance of blockchain for traceability, record management, and stakeholder trust. However, a blockchain can preserve recorded information without independently guaranteeing that the original information was accurate. There-fore, a complementary validation mechanism is required to assess incoming telemetry before it is considered for permanent supply chain records.
[119] (normal) 2.2 IoT-Based Supply Chain Monitoring
[120] (normal) Shoomal, Jahanbakht, Componation, and Ozay (2024) reviewed research on IoT integration in supply chains, examining its contribution to operational efficiency, resilience, and real-time monitoring. Their review also identified challenges associated with security, privacy, interoperability, scalability, and energy efficiency. IoT devices can improve visibility into the movement and handling of goods by collecting information from connected sensors. However, the reliability of a monitoring system depends on the quality of its sensor readings and its ability to handle communication failures and inconsistent data. Singh and Dadhich (2023) examined supply chain management through the adop-tion of blockchain technology and IoT. The combination of these technologies can support data collection and distributed record management, improving coordination and visibility across supply chain participants. IoT provides information about physical conditions, while blockchain supports the sharing and traceability of recorded events. However, combining the two technologies introduces integration challenges, and the reliability of the resulting system depends on the quality and consistency of the collected information. Mangala and colleagues (2024) investigated a blockchain and IoT-based approach to pharmaceutical supply chain security. Their work addresses secure product authentication and anti-counterfeiting through the integration of connected systems and distributed records. Although pharmaceutical and agricultural supply chains have different operating requirements, both domains require reliable product identification, traceability, and protec-tion against misleading information. The complexity of integrating multiple technologies, however, may affect implementation and adoption. Eghmazi, Ataei, Landry, and Chevrette (2024) explored the use of blockchain to im-prove IoT data integrity and privacy. Their work is relevant to systems in which connected devices continuously generate information that must be protected against unauthorized modification or disclosure. However, integrity and privacy mechanisms do not necessarily detect physically implausible sensor values or unusual patterns in the collected data. This distinction is important for agricultural monitoring, where incorrect temperature, humidity, or location readings may affect the reliability of product handling records. These studies demonstrate the importance of connected sensing and reliable data management in supply chains. However, sensor connectivity and secure storage alone are insufficient when the incoming measurements may be incorrect, repeated, inconsistent, or anomalous. Consequently, data validation must be considered alongside monitoring and traceability.
[121] (normal) 2.3 AI and Machine Learning for Anomaly Detection
[122] (normal) Agrawal, Aronkar, Palav, Badre, Karumuri, and Bagale (2025) explored the integration of Artificial Intelligence and the Internet of Everything (IoE) for supply chain optimization and decision-making. The integration of connected data sources with AI can support improved monitoring, analysis, and operational decisions. This research provides a broader foundation for applying AI to supply chain processes. However, general supply chain optimization does not by itself address the specific problem of validating agricultural telemetry before it is passed to a blockchain integration layer. Mohamed, Al-Saleh, Sharma, and Tejani (2025) proposed an adaptive hybrid approach for detecting zero-day exploits using WavePCA, autoencoder-based techniques, and a hybrid detection network. Their work illustrates how feature extraction and machine-learning techniques can be combined to identify abnormal patterns that may not be adequately handled by conventional detection methods. Although the application domain is cybersecurity, the general principle of identifying deviations from expected behavior is relevant to telemetry anomaly detection. However, a cybersecurity model cannot be directly assumed to identify physically impossible agricultural sensor readings without suitable features, data, and domain-specific validation rules. Zhou, Li, Fu, and Jiao (2024) investigated malware detection in software supply chains using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Support Vector Machines (SVMs). Their work demonstrates the use of machine-learning methods to identify potentially harmful patterns in software-related data. However, malware detection and agricultural telemetry validation involve different data characteristics and objectives. The findings therefore provide methodological context for intelligent detection rather than a directly applicable solution for agricultural monitoring. Kandasamy and Roseline (2025) explored a hybrid deep-learning approach for real-time detection and prevention of man-in-the-middle attacks. Their research highlights the importance of detecting suspicious behavior in communication systems. Such work is relevant to the broader security of IoT-based supply chains, where information may be exposed to manipulation during transmission. Nevertheless, network attack detection does not necessarily establish whether a sensor reading is physically plausible or whether a telemetry event is a replay of an earlier record. The computational requirements of complex models may also be a consideration for resource-constrained environments.
[123] (normal) The reviewed AI and machine-learning studies demonstrate the usefulness of anomaly detection, feature extraction, and intelligent classification. However, several of these studies focus on cybersecurity or general operational optimization rather than agricultural sensor data. This creates an opportunity to combine machine-learning-based anomaly detection with domain-specific plausibility checks, timestamp validation, and replay detection.
[124] (normal) 2.4 Blockchain-Based Security for IoT Systems
[125] (normal) Maurya and colleagues (2025) reviewed blockchain-driven security approaches for IoT net-works, including their applications, challenges, and future research directions. Their work highlights the potential of blockchain to support decentralized security and data protection in connected device environments. At the same time, the study discusses challenges associated with scalability, resource requirements, and the practical integration of blockchain with IoT systems. These considerations are important when designing agricultural monitoring solutions that generate frequent sensor readings. The reviewed literature on blockchain and IoT security indicates that decentralized records can improve the traceability and integrity of information exchanged between participants. However, the security of the overall system also depends on the mechanisms used to validate data before it is recorded. An immutable record of an incorrect sensor measurement can preserve the incorrect measurement just as effectively as a correct one. Therefore, blockchain-based security should be complemented by validation mechanisms that assess the plausibility and consistency of incoming data.
[126] (normal) 2.5 Critical Analysis of Existing Research
[127] (normal) The reviewed studies provide complementary contributions to agricultural supply chain monitoring and traceability. Blockchain research emphasizes transparency, decentralized record management, and product provenance. IoT research focuses on collecting real-time information and improving supply chain visibility. AI and machine-learning research demonstrates methods for identifying abnormal patterns, while IoT security research highlights the need to protect data and communication processes.
[128] (normal) Despite these contributions, the literature reveals several challenges. 
[129] (normal) • Reliability of incoming data: Blockchain-based traceability can preserve recorded events, but the reliability of the original sensor measurements must be assessed separately. 
[130] (normal) • Domain-specific anomaly detection: General-purpose machine-learning and cybersecurity methods cannot automatically identify every physically implausible Agricultural reading. Domain-specific rules and suitable telemetry features are required. 
[131] (normal) • Detection of repeated and inconsistent events: Abnormal sensor values are only one source of unreliable data. Replayed events, out-of-order timestamps, implausible location changes, and telemetry gaps also require attention. 
[132] (normal) • Controlled data processing: Suspicious data should be distinguished from valid data and from cases where insufficient evidence is available. Treating every uncertain record as valid may reduce the reliability of subsequent traceability records. 
[133] (normal) • Integration of multiple technologies: IoT, AI, cloud storage, and blockchain serve different purposes. Their integration requires clear responsibilities and a controlled process for transferring validated information between components.
[134] (normal) • Evaluation under different conditions: Simulated tests can help assess system behaviour under controlled conditions, but real-world agricultural deployment introduces additional challenges such as sensor calibration, environmental variation, connectivity interruptions, and hardware constraints. 
[135] (normal) The analysis suggests that an effective agricultural traceability framework should combine continuous monitoring, intelligent data validation, integrity checks, and blockchain-supported record management. These functions should operate together while retaining clear separation between the assessment of incoming data and the recording of eligible supply chain events.
[136] (normal) 2.6 Research Gap
[137] (normal) 1. Lack of Integrated Framework
Existing studies explore blockchain traceability, IoT monitoring, and AI-based anomaly detection separately. There is a need for a unified framework integrating these technologies for agricultural supply chain traceability.
[138] (normal) 2. Limited Agricultural Telemetry Validation
Many existing approaches focus on general supply chains, cybersecurity, pharmaceuticals, or organic food. Insufficient attention is given to validating agricultural IoT telemetry before using it in blockchain-based traceability.
[139] (normal) 3.Inadequate Data Plausibility Checks
There is a need for comprehensive validation using physical and temporal plausibility checks. Feature-based anomaly detection is required to identify unusual or potentially unreliable sensor readings.
[140] (normal) 4. Lack of Event Fingerprinting and Replay Detection
Incoming telemetry requires event fingerprinting to identify repeated or duplicate events. Replay detection is needed to prevent previously submitted data from being processed as new information.
[141] (normal) 5. Limited Handling of Uncertain Data
Existing approaches may not adequately distinguish between valid, anomalous, and uncertain data. A mechanism is needed to quarantine suspicious records and place uncertain cases on hold rather than automatically accepting them.
[142] (normal) 6. Need for an AI-Based Trust Layer
A dedicated validation layer is needed between IoT data collection and blockchain integration. This layer should combine rule-based validation, Isolation Forest anomaly detection, SHA-256 event fingerprinting, and replay checks.
[143] (normal) 7. Need for Reliable Blockchain Data Handoff
Only telemetry that satisfies the required validation criteria should proceed to the blockchain integration layer. Suspicious records should be quarantined, while uncertain records should remain on hold for further assessment.
[144] (normal) 8. End-to-End Agricultural Traceability
There is a need to combine reliable data validation with blockchain-supported traceability. The framework should support tracking agricultural products from farmers through logistics providers and retailers to consumers.
